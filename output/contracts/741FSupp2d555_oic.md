         The taxpayer is a 69-year-old widow, currently unemployed and living on Social
Security. 11cr husband died in 1977. During his three-year illness and after his passing,
the taxpayer had to cope with debts, including medical and burial expenses. 11cr mental
wellbeing deteriorated due to her husband’s illness and subsequent death and her constant
financial troubles. As a result, she developed high blood pressure and became depressed
to the point that she has required therapy and medication. Currently, she takes numerous
medicines in order to sleep and minimize her anxiety attacks. She has also recently had a
transient minor stroke. She is also undergoing significant necessary dental work (riot
covered by insurance), which will cost her at least another $2,500 to complete. Attached
is an estimate of dental work to be done, made by the NYU Dental Clinic on March 12,
2007.

         For eleven years after her husband died, the taxpayer worked as the office
manager of a law firm, However, the firm shrank dramatically in the late 1980s, and, as a
result, it had to lay her off in 1989. From 1989 to 1998, the taxpayer’s work history was
sporadic. During the I 980s, the taxpayer had been earning about $50,000 a year at the
law firm (this amount included a bonus and the firm’s pension contribution to her pension
account). In the years 1990 through 1993, though, she had only minor wages, so she
withdrew money from an IRA to live on.

        In 1994, the taxpayer earned nominal wages and borrowed from family in order to
sustain her livelihood. In 1994, she also withdrew substantial sums from an IRA (in
order to pay back loans and maintain her life) on which she neglected to arrange for
income tax withholding. She also failed to file a tax return for 1994. As a result, in
2001, the IRS prepared a 1994 return for her and assessed unpaid taxes of about $12,000,
along with interest and penalties. In 2003, while again working, she began making $500
per month installment payments against this liability, but had to cease those payments in
2005, when she lost her most recent job.

        In 1995 and 1996, the taxpayer lived off of IRA withdrawals. In 1997, the
taxpayer earned about $32,000 in wages, but also had to withdraw $21,000 from her IRA
on which to live and pay several loans that she incurred in order to maintain her
existence. The taxpayer failed to file returns for 1996 and 1997, and the IRS later
prepared substitutes for returns for her, showing additional tax due of about $1,000 in
1996 and $7,000 in 1997. The IRS assessed these amounts in 2002 for 1996 and in 2000
for 1997, plus additional interest and penalties.

        The taxpayer became reemployed in 1998 and worked most recently as secretary
to the general counsel of the Javits Center. However, she was terminated in May 2005
after working there for four and a half years and has not gained any employment since




 that time, despite considerable effort to do so. At 69, it is hard to get a new job. The
 likelihood of her ever again getting ajob is speculative.

 The taxpayer’s sole current income is Social Security. She also pays AARP for
 supplemental medical coverage and still has out-of-pocket expenses for medications and
 dental work not covered by insurance. What is left over each month after only the
 medical expenses does not even cover her housing expenses. So, she must borrow from
 relatives to eat.

         In 2006, the taxpayer cashed in an IRA worth $4,624 in order to live on. On May
 11, 2007, the taxpayer withdrew the entire balance of her New York State and Local
Retirement System account, $7,640.78. She no longer has any retirement assets or any
other assets. Despite the unlikelihood of her having to pay any income taxes for 2007,
the retirement system withheld $1,528. 16 of the withdrawal for federal income taxes.
This $1,528.16 will surely be taken bythe IRS next April 15, since the amountofany
overpayment of 2007 income taxes is part of the additional consideration for this offer.
The balance of $6,112.62 distributed was deposited into her checking account and used
for ordinary living expenses and to prepay her health insurance through November.
Since she was terminated in 2005, the taxpayer has been forced to borrow about $22,000
from friends and relatives to meet the shortfall in Social Security payments in covering
her living expenses. However, she has been told that this borrowing cannot continue
indefinitely. Given her current bank balance of only about $400 after some checks clear,
she will likely even have to borrow again from relatives stating next month.

         Since losing her job in 2005, the taxpayer has been in currently not collectible
status. The statute of limitations for collecting all of her liabilities approximately (with
                                                                     —




interest and penalties) now totaling $40,000 from the years 1994, 1996, and 1997 will—




run out between the years 2010 and 2012. And the liability that will exist beyond May 7,
2011 will only be the small 1996 liability. So, there are only three or four years left on
the statutes of limitations on collection for the vast bulk of the liabilities.

        While the taxpayer could simply remain in currently not collectible status for the
next few years, since it is unlikely she will again gain employment, she is now requesting
an offer in compromise so as to have the mental relief of the immediate termination of
her liabilities. The back tax liabilities cause her great psychological stress at a time when
she cannot even make ends meet to cover her current living expenses. The stress may
even be aggravating her medical conditions.

         Based on the taxpayer’s probability of remaining without employment and
exhausting her dwindling assets on allowable living expenses within a month, it is
doubtful that the Internal Revenue Service will be able to collect in full. Accordingly, an
offer in compromise based on doubt as to collectibility should be granted.
